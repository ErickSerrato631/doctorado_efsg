\section{Introduction}

Tumor--immune dynamics are open, driven, and spatially heterogeneous. From a nonequilibrium-physics perspective, this makes tumor progression less a question of convergence to a single homogeneous equilibrium and more a question of which transient routes, interfaces, and spatial scales are selected by the local interactions. Spatial reaction--diffusion models of tumor growth and treatment provide a natural language for this problem \cite{murray2002,enderling2014,chaplain2006}: local growth and competition determine the kinetic flow, while diffusion controls the relaxation of spatial gradients and the cost of interfaces.

Classical reciprocal Lotka--Volterra or logistic descriptions are useful baselines, but they do not fully capture two mechanisms that are relevant for cancer--immune competition. First, cooperative tumor growth can generate an Allee-type response, where growth depends nonlinearly on local tumor density and may introduce sharp viability barriers \cite{gerlee2022,bottger2015}. Second, the action of cancer on immune or healthy tissue is not generally the mirror image of the reverse action: immune cytotoxicity, tissue damage, and immune recruitment need not be pairwise reciprocal \cite{delitala2007,gatenby2004,fruchart2021}. Such nonreciprocity can introduce rotational components in phase space, reorganize nullclines, and bias spatial routes even when homogeneous reference states are linearly unstable.

Here we study a three-field cancer--healthy--immune reaction--diffusion model with a cooperative strong Allee effect, a binary reciprocity parameter $\mathcal{R}$, and a spatially targeted Hill immunological control field. The case $\mathcal{R}=0$ is treated as the nonreciprocal baseline, while $\mathcal{R}=1$ represents a reciprocal-completion setting that is closer to a free-energy-gradient structure. We compare the four strong-Allee scenarios obtained by switching $\mathcal{R}$ and the Hill control on or off. The analysis focuses on three observables: spatial correlation lengths, effective population potentials, and diffusive entropy production. Together these quantities show whether control changes the geometry of tumor--immune organization or merely produces a secondary correction to a route already selected by reciprocity structure.
